# Project Proposal

## Electricity Demand Forecasting Using Statistical, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning Methods

## 1. Introduction

Electricity demand changes continuously according to factors such as time of day, day of the week, weather conditions, holidays, public events, and seasonal patterns. Electricity providers must maintain a balance between electricity generation and consumption. Underestimating demand may result in power shortages, while overestimating demand may increase operating costs and waste resources.

Accurate electricity demand forecasting can support power-generation planning, peak-demand management, maintenance scheduling, energy conservation, and efficient resource allocation. Traditional statistical forecasting methods are useful for modelling trend, seasonality, and autocorrelation. Machine learning models can capture nonlinear relationships between electricity demand, historical consumption, calendar variables, and weather conditions. Deep learning models such as Long Short-Term Memory networks can learn complex sequential patterns directly from historical observations.

This project proposes the development of a short-term electricity demand forecasting system that compares statistical time series models, traditional machine learning models, and deep learning models. The comparison will determine whether the additional complexity and training time of deep learning provide a meaningful improvement over simpler forecasting approaches.

## 2. Project Title

**Electricity Demand Forecasting Using Statistical, Machine Learning, and Deep Learning Methods**

## 3. Problem Statement

Electricity consumption is highly dynamic and influenced by multiple temporal and environmental factors. Simple forecasting methods may not adequately capture daily cycles, weekly patterns, seasonal changes, nonlinear weather effects, and long-term dependencies.

Statistical models such as ARIMA and SARIMA are effective for modelling linear temporal patterns and seasonality. Machine learning models such as XGBoost can use engineered lag, rolling, calendar, and weather features to capture nonlinear relationships. Deep learning models such as LSTM can process sequential data and learn long-term dependencies.

However, deep learning models require more computational resources, longer training times, and more complex parameter tuning. Therefore, it is important to determine whether deep learning produces significantly better forecasting accuracy than traditional methods for the selected electricity demand dataset.

The main research problem is:

> Which forecasting approach—statistical modelling, traditional machine learning, or deep learning—provides the best balance of accuracy, computational efficiency, and interpretability for short-term electricity demand forecasting?

## 4. Aim

The aim of this project is to develop and evaluate an electricity demand forecasting system that compares statistical, machine learning, and deep learning methods using historical electricity consumption, weather information, and calendar-related variables.

The project will answer the following questions:

1. What daily, weekly, and seasonal patterns exist in electricity demand?

2. How strongly do weather conditions influence electricity consumption?

3. Which historical lag periods are most useful for forecasting future demand?

## 7. Proposed Dataset

### 7.1 Primary Dataset

The primary dataset selected for this project is the **Load Forecasting Dataset** published on Kaggle (`isuranga/load-forecasting-dataset`), covering Sri Lanka from January 2020 to May 2025 at 15-minute resolution (resampled to hourly for this project). It provides electricity demand alongside weather observations (temperature, humidity, wind speed, rainfall, solar irradiance) and calendar variables on a matching timestamp, removing the need for a separate data-fusion step, and its ~5.4 years of continuous coverage is sufficient for daily, weekly, and seasonal pattern analysis.

**Data authenticity note:** the Kaggle listing describes this dataset as sourced from the Ceylon Electricity Board (CEB). Inspection of the data during the data-understanding stage (`01_data_understanding.ipynb`) found several inconsistencies with that claim: demand values average ~1,500 kW, roughly three orders of magnitude below Sri Lanka's actual national peak grid demand of ~2,500–2,700 MW; the bundled "GDP (LKR)" column fluctuates narrowly around 1,000 per row, far below Sri Lanka's actual nominal GDP (tens of trillions of LKR); the `Season` column's month mapping does not correspond to Sri Lanka's tropical/monsoon climate or any standard hemisphere convention; and the data has zero missing values or duplicate timestamps across 5.4 years, which is unusual for raw utility telemetry. Taken together, this indicates the dataset is synthetically generated rather than authentic CEB grid readings. It is used in this project as a **synthetic Sri Lanka-style dataset** — realistic enough in structure and scale (per-observation ranges, daily/weekly/seasonal patterns, weather coupling) to demonstrate and compare the statistical, machine-learning, and deep-learning forecasting methods this project is built around — but its outputs should not be read as validated real-world Sri Lankan demand forecasts. See Section 28 (Limitations) for how this affects interpretation of results.

## 10. Forecasting Models

### 10.1 Baseline Models

Baseline models will establish the minimum forecasting performance.

The proposed baseline methods are:

* Previous-value naïve forecast
* Previous-day forecast
* Seasonal naïve forecast
* Moving-average forecast

A seasonal naïve forecast may predict electricity demand using the demand recorded at the same hour on the previous day or previous week.

### 10.2 ARIMA

ARIMA will be used to model autoregressive relationships, differencing, and moving-average behaviour.

The model will be represented by:

* Autoregressive order
* Differencing order
* Moving-average order

### 10.3 SARIMA

SARIMA will extend ARIMA by including seasonal components.

It may be used to model:

* Daily seasonality
* Weekly seasonality
* Longer recurring demand patterns

### 10.4 Random Forest

Random Forest will use engineered features such as:

* Lag demand
* Rolling demand
* Hour
* Day of week
* Weather conditions

It will also provide feature-importance values.

### 10.5 XGBoost

XGBoost will be the primary traditional machine learning model.

It is suitable because it can:

* Capture nonlinear relationships
* Handle interactions between variables
* Use lag and rolling features
* Model weather effects
* Provide feature importance
* Support efficient hyperparameter tuning

### 10.6 Long Short-Term Memory Network

LSTM will be the main deep learning model.

LSTM is designed to learn patterns in sequential data and may capture both short-term and long-term dependencies.

The LSTM input will use sequences created through a sliding-window method.

For example, a model may use the previous 24 or 48 hours of electricity demand and weather variables to predict the next hour or next 24 hours.

The proposed LSTM architecture may include:

* Input sequence layer
* One or two LSTM layers
* Dropout layer
* Fully connected output layer
* Adam optimizer
* Mean squared error loss function

Important parameters will include:

* Sequence length
* Number of LSTM units
* Number of layers
* Batch size
* Learning rate
* Number of epochs
* Dropout rate

Early stopping will be used to reduce overfitting and unnecessary training.

### 10.7 Optional GRU Model

A Gated Recurrent Unit model may be included as an optional extension.

GRU has a simpler architecture than LSTM and may train faster while producing similar forecasting accuracy.

## 14. Time Series Cross-Validation

Walk-forward or expanding-window validation will be used.

The process will involve:

1. Training the model on an earlier period.
2. Predicting the following period.
3. Expanding the training window.
4. Repeating the procedure.
5. Averaging performance across validation periods.

This approach reflects how forecasting models operate in real applications.

## 16. Evaluation Metrics

The models will be evaluated using:

### Mean Absolute Error

MAE calculates the average absolute difference between actual and predicted demand.

### Root Mean Squared Error

RMSE gives more importance to large forecasting errors.

### Mean Absolute Percentage Error

MAPE expresses forecast errors as percentages.

### Symmetric Mean Absolute Percentage Error

SMAPE may be used as an additional percentage-based metric.

### Mean Absolute Scaled Error

MASE may be included to compare the models directly with a naïve forecast.

### Statistical Significance Testing

Since several research questions ask whether one model *significantly* outperforms another (for example, LSTM versus XGBoost and SARIMA), forecast errors will also be compared using the Diebold-Mariano test. This test evaluates whether the difference in forecast accuracy between two models is statistically significant rather than attributable to random variation, and it will be applied to the relevant model pairs identified in the research questions.

### Computational Measures

The project will also record:

* Training time
* Prediction time
* Memory usage, where practical
* Number of model parameters
* Complexity of implementation

## 21. Peak-Demand Analysis

A peak-demand period will be defined as any hour in which observed demand exceeds the 95th percentile of demand for the corresponding season, rather than a single fixed value across the whole year. This seasonal threshold prevents naturally high winter or summer demand from dominating the definition of a "peak" and keeps the comparison consistent across all models.

The project will:

* Define a peak-demand threshold
* Identify actual peak-demand periods
* Compare predicted and actual peaks
* Calculate peak-demand forecasting error
* Examine whether weather and calendar factors contribute to peaks
* Compare each model’s performance during high-demand periods

This analysis is important because large errors during peak periods may have greater practical consequences than small errors during normal periods.

## 28. Limitations

**Dataset authenticity:** as noted in Section 7.1, the primary dataset appears to be synthetically generated rather than authentic Ceylon Electricity Board telemetry, despite being marketed as CEB-sourced on Kaggle. This project therefore demonstrates and compares forecasting methodologies on a realistic, Sri Lanka-style synthetic series rather than validated real-world national demand data. Model comparisons (accuracy, training time, interpretability) remain methodologically valid, but absolute forecast values and any claims about actual Sri Lankan grid behaviour should not be drawn from these results without separately validating against authentic CEB data.

Other possible limitations include:

* Missing electricity-demand observations
* Incomplete weather variables
* Limited historical coverage
* Unrecorded public events
* Sudden policy or behavioural changes
* Power cuts and supply restrictions
* Extreme weather conditions
* High computational cost of deep learning tuning
* LSTM overfitting
* Reduced deep learning interpretability
* Difficulty forecasting unusual peak-demand periods

## 34. Conclusion

This project will develop a comprehensive electricity demand forecasting system using statistical time series methods, traditional machine learning, and deep learning.

The project will compare naïve forecasting, ARIMA or SARIMA, XGBoost, and LSTM models. Each method will be evaluated using forecasting accuracy, training time, prediction speed, model complexity, interpretability, and performance during peak-demand periods.

The inclusion of deep learning will allow the project to investigate whether LSTM can capture complex sequential dependencies more effectively than traditional approaches. At the same time, the comparison will determine whether any accuracy improvement justifies the higher computational cost and reduced interpretability.

The final outcome will provide a practical and evidence-based recommendation regarding the most suitable approach for short-term electricity demand forecasting.
